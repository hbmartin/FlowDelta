# Visualizing Control-Flow and Data-Flow Changes Across Code Edits: State of the Art and Foundations

The single most important finding: there is **no mature, widely-adopted tool that visualizes source-level control-flow-graph (CFG) or data-flow/dependence-graph deltas as first-class node-link diffs for developers** — the field is strong in the two adjacent layers (AST/tree differencing and dynamic-graph visualization) but weak precisely at the intersection you're asking about, and the best-developed CFG-delta *visualizers* that exist are binary reverse-engineering tools (Google/Zynamics BinDiff, BinHunt), not source-editing tools.

## TL;DR
- The field splits into three loosely-connected layers: (1) **structural/syntactic differencing** (tree/AST diffing — ChangeDistiller, GumTree, Diff/TS, MTDIFF), which is mature and productized; (2) **semantic/behavioral differencing** over control- and data-flow representations (PDG/SDG/CFG diffing, Semantic Diff, JDiff, Differential Symbolic Execution, SymDiff), which is scientifically deep but largely research-grade; and (3) **graph-diff visualization** (dynamic-graph visualization from IEEE VIS/TVCG — GraphDiaries, AniMatrix), which is well-developed in InfoVis but rarely wired directly to program CFG/PDG deltas.
- The closest production examples of CFG/call-graph *delta* visualization are binary-diffing tools (Google/Zynamics **BinDiff**, **BinHunt**) that match and color CFG/call-graph changes via graph isomorphism, and AST-diff tools (**Difftastic**, **GumTree**) that operate on trees, not dependence graphs.
- For a practitioner building modern tooling, the actionable synthesis is: use **GumTree/ChangeDistiller-style AST matching** to establish node correspondences, lift to a **PDG/SDG** for control/data dependence, compute a **graph delta** with isomorphism/matching plus edit-script semantics, and render it with **staged animated transitions and add/remove/change color encoding** drawn from the dynamic-graph-visualization literature (GraphDiaries).

## Key Findings

**1. Textual diff is the baseline everyone is trying to escape.** The Unix `diff` family rests on the longest-common-subsequence / shortest-edit-script formulation, with the Myers O(ND) algorithm (Eugene Myers, "An O(ND) Difference Algorithm and Its Variations," *Algorithmica*, 1986) as the canonical engine. Its limitations — line granularity, no move/update operations, no alignment to program structure — are the explicit motivation for every structural and semantic approach that follows.

**2. Tree/AST differencing is the mature, productized layer.** The foundational algorithm is Chawathe, Rajaraman, Garcia-Molina & Widom, "Change Detection in Hierarchically Structured Information" (SIGMOD 1996), which framed hierarchical diffing as minimum-cost edit-script computation with insert/delete/update/move on trees. ChangeDistiller (Fluri, Würsch, Pinzger, Gall, *IEEE TSE* 2007) adapted Chawathe to ASTs for fine-grained source change extraction. GumTree (Falleri, Morandat, Blanc, Martinez, Monperrus, ASE 2014) is the de facto modern standard, using a two-phase greedy top-down (isomorphic subtree anchoring) + bottom-up matching that recovers move actions. MTDIFF (Dotzler & Philippsen, ASE 2016) and the 2024 "Fine-grained, Accurate, and Scalable" GumTree revision improve move detection and scalability.

**3. Semantic/dependence differencing is the scientifically deep layer.** Horwitz's "Identifying the Semantic and Textual Differences Between Two Versions of a Program" (PLDI 1990) is the seminal work: it uses a Program Representation Graph to partition components into semantically-changed vs. merely textually-changed. Jackson & Ladd's "Semantic Diff" (ICSM 1994) summarizes changes in terms of input-output dependences per procedure. These sit on the PDG (Ferrante, Ottenstein, Warren, *TOPLAS* 1987) and its interprocedural extension the SDG (Horwitz, Reps, Binkley, PLDI 1988), the same structures underlying program slicing (Weiser, 1981/1984).

**4. Behavioral/verification-based diffing is a distinct, powerful branch.** Differential Symbolic Execution (Person, Dwyer, Elbaum, Păsăreanu, FSE 2008) characterizes behavioral deltas using symbolic summaries and uninterpreted functions for unchanged code. SymDiff (Lahiri, Hawblitzel, Kawaguchi, Rebêlo, CAV 2012) does language-agnostic equivalence checking over the Boogie IVL. ARDiff (Badihi, Akinotcho, Li, Rubin, ESEC/FSE 2020) scales equivalence checking via abstraction-refinement of common code.

**5. Graph-diff visualization is mature in InfoVis but under-applied to programs.** The dynamic-graph-visualization survey (Beck, Burch, Diehl, Weiskopf, *CGF* 2017) taxonomizes the entire space into animation vs. timeline approaches. GraphDiaries (Bach, Pietriga, Fekete, *IEEE TVCG* 2014) is the key technique for staged animated transitions of node/edge add/remove/change. In the software-specific space, BinDiff/BinHunt visualize CFG/call-graph deltas for reverse engineering, and AniMatrix (VISSOFT 2014) animates matrix-based software-evolution deltas.

## Details

### Subtopic 1 — Semantic / structural code differencing (tree- and graph-based)

**Baselines and their limits.** Textual differencing descends from file-comparison work (Miller & Myers, "A File Comparison Program," *Software: Practice and Experience*, 1985) and the LCS/shortest-edit-script model; the workhorse is Eugene W. Myers' O(ND) diff algorithm (*Algorithmica* 1(2), 1986), which underlies GNU diff and Git's default diff. Git also ships a Histogram/patience variant. The universally-cited limitations, stated crisply in the GumTree paper: diff "only computes additions and deletions and does not consider actions such as update and move," and "works at a granularity (the text line) that is both coarse grain and not aligned with the source code structure."

**Foundational tree edit distance.** The classical ordered-tree edit distance algorithm is Zhang & Shasha, "Simple Fast Algorithms for the Editing Distance Between Trees and Related Problems" (*SIAM J. Computing* 18(6), 1989). RTED (Robust Tree Edit Distance, Pawlik & Augsten) is a later optimal baseline used for comparison. Optimal tree edit distance is expensive (roughly cubic), which is why source-code tools use heuristics.

**Chawathe et al. (SIGMOD 1996)** — "Change Detection in Hierarchically Structured Information." Defined the hierarchical change-detection problem as finding a minimum-cost edit script (insert, delete, update, plus subtree move) transforming one data tree into another, with domain heuristics (e.g., FastMatch) beating general-purpose algorithms. This is the direct ancestor of both ChangeDistiller and (via the "MH-Diff" line) several later tools.

**ChangeDistiller (Fluri, Würsch, Pinzger, Gall, *IEEE TSE* 33(11), 2007)** — "Change Distilling: Tree Differencing for Fine-Grained Source Code Change Extraction." Improved Chawathe's matching (using bigram string similarity for leaves and a subtree-similarity criterion for inner nodes) to extract fine-grained changes classified by change type. Per the authors, "our algorithm approximates the minimum edit script 45 percent better than the original change extraction approach by Chawathe et al.... we reach a mean absolute percentage error of 34 percent, compared to the 79 percent reached by the original algorithm," evaluated on a benchmark of 1,064 manually classified changes across 219 revisions from three open-source projects. Widely used in software-evolution mining.

**GumTree (Falleri, Morandat, Blanc, Martinez, Monperrus, ASE 2014)** — "Fine-grained and Accurate Source Code Differencing," pp. 313–324, DOI 10.1145/2642937.2642982. Two phases: (1) greedy top-down search for isomorphic subtrees of decreasing height, establishing "anchor" mappings; (2) bottom-up matching where two nodes match if a sufficient fraction (Dice coefficient) of their descendants are already matched, followed by an optimal recovery step on small subtrees. Produces insert/delete/update/**move** actions on ASTs. The reference implementation supports many languages via pluggable parsers (Eclipse JDT, Rhino, cgum, etc.). It is the single most influential modern tool; the Spoon-based `gumtree-spoon-ast-diff` is a popular Java variant.

**Diff/TS (Hashimoto & Mori, 2008)** — performs fine-grained structural analysis of source trees across Python, Java, C, C++, and is often cited as an early multi-language AST-diff that visualizes structural edit actions. (Exact venue reported at lower confidence — see Caveats.)

**MTDIFF (Dotzler & Philippsen, ASE 2016)** — "Move-optimized source code tree differencing." Adds five general optimizations (applicable to GumTree, RTED, JSync, ChangeDistiller) that shorten edit scripts, plus a ChangeDistiller-derived algorithm with better move detection; shortened scripts for a large fraction of changes across nine repositories.

**Current state / newer work.** The 2024 extension "Fine-grained, Accurate, and Scalable Source Code Differencing" (Falleri & Martinez, ICSE 2024 / *IEEE TSE*) replaces GumTree's expensive optimal recovery step with a cheaper heuristic; the authors report the 'simple' heuristic "achieves a median size 50% smaller on all datasets, and a total speedup between 50x and 281x compared to the default opt-1000," validated against four datasets in two languages. SatDiff (arXiv 2024) reformulates tree diffing as MaxSAT for minimal, type-safe edit scripts. HyperDiff (Dilavrec et al.) addresses diffing across large code histories. A 2021 ICSE differential-testing study (Fan et al.) found GumTree, MTDIFF, and IJM each produce inaccurate mappings in 20–36% of file revisions — the accuracy of AST matching remains an open problem.

**Production AST-diff tools.** Difftastic (Wilfred Hughes) parses with tree-sitter and runs a structural diff, ignoring formatting-only changes and detecting moves; supports 30–50+ languages and integrates as a Git external diff. Diffsitter is a similar tree-sitter+LCS tool. These are syntactic (tree-level), not dependence-graph-level.

### Subtopic 2 — Control-flow graph (CFG) differencing and visualization

**JDiff (Apiwattanapong, Orso, Harrold)** is the canonical source-level CFG-diff work — first at ASE 2004 ("A Differencing Algorithm for Object-Oriented Programs") and expanded in *Automated Software Engineering* 14(1):3–36, 2007 ("JDiff: A differencing technique and tool for object-oriented programs"). It introduces an **Enhanced Control Flow Graph (ECFG)** that models object-oriented features (dynamic dispatch, exception handling, synchronization), then matches nodes between two versions' ECFGs — hammock-node decomposition plus recursive matching — to identify and classify statement-level changes. It's designed to feed program-profile estimation, impact analysis, and regression testing, capturing behavioral differences (e.g., a call binding to a different method) that plain `diff` misses.

**Binary CFG diffing (production).** BinDiff (Halvar Flake / Thomas Dullien & Rolf Rolles, "Graph-based comparison of executable objects," 2004/2005; now Google/Zynamics) matches functions across binaries via a variant of graph isomorphism over CFGs and call graphs, using structural attributes (function content hash, loop count, string references) as anchors, and offers a **visual diff that colors corresponding nodes in identical colors**. BinHunt (Gao, Reiter, Song, 2008) improves on BinDiff with symbolic execution + theorem proving to match semantically-equivalent basic blocks and a backtracking graph-isomorphism algorithm, yielding a 0–1 semantic difference score. Later work (Diaphora, DeepBinDiff, QBinDiff) reframes diffing as graph alignment or uses learned embeddings. These are the most mature CFG-delta *visualizers* in existence, though aimed at reverse engineers, not authors editing source.

**Evaluation.** There is explicit work on "A Method to Evaluate CFG Comparison Algorithms," reflecting that matching quality (isomorphism heuristics vs. ground truth) is itself a research subject.

### Subtopic 3 — Data-flow / dependence differencing

**The representations.** The PDG (Ferrante, Ottenstein, Warren, *TOPLAS* 9(3):319–349, 1987) makes explicit both **data dependences** and **control dependences** (derived from the CFG via post-dominance) for each operation. The SDG (Horwitz, Reps, Binkley, "Interprocedural Slicing Using Dependence Graphs," PLDI 1988 / *TOPLAS* 1990) extends this across procedure boundaries with call/parameter nodes and summary edges. These are the substrate for all dependence-based differencing.

**Horwitz (PLDI 1990)** — "Identifying the Semantic and Textual Differences Between Two Versions of a Program." Uses a Program Representation Graph (a PDG variant) and a partitioning algorithm to classify each component of the new version as **unchanged**, **textually changed** (semantically equivalent but different text), or **semantically changed** (different flow/control-dependence behavior). This is arguably the seminal "meaning-aware diff." It grew out of the Horwitz–Prins–Reps program-integration work ("Integrating Non-Interfering Versions of Programs," *TOPLAS* 1989), which used PDG/SDG comparison to detect interference when merging variants.

**Jackson & Ladd, "Semantic Diff" (ICSM 1994)** — takes two versions of a procedure and reports differences in terms of **observable input-output dependences** (which outputs depend on which inputs), rather than syntactic structure. Because the analysis is semantic, it requires no prior syntactic matching and correctly reports that meaning-preserving transformations (e.g., local variable renaming) have no visible effect. This is the direct conceptual ancestor of behavioral/semantic diffing.

**Dependence-based change impact.** Forward slicing from modified statements propagates along dependence edges to find potentially-affected program points (regression-test selection); backward slicing supports debugging. Slice-based change-impact analysis remains an active area (e.g., regression-test prioritization work through the mid-2020s).

### Subtopic 4 — Semantic differencing of programs more broadly

**Differential Symbolic Execution (DSE)** — Person, Dwyer, Elbaum, Păsăreanu, FSE 2008, pp. 226–237, DOI 10.1145/1453101.1453131. Collects symbolic summaries of both versions, replaces unchanged code fragments with **uninterpreted functions** to reduce cost, then compares summaries to compute a **behavioral delta**: the input partitions for which outputs differ, or a proof of equivalence. Followed by directed incremental symbolic execution (Person et al., PLDI 2011) and iProperty-style change-aware checking.

**SymDiff** — Lahiri, Hawblitzel, Kawaguchi, Rebêlo, CAV 2012, LNCS 7358, pp. 712–717. Language-agnostic equivalence checking and semantic-difference display over the Boogie intermediate verification language (front-ends for C, C#, x86), using Z3. Related MSR line: Differential Assertion Checking (Lahiri, McMillan, Sharma, Hawblitzel, ESEC/FSE 2013) and Verification Modulo Versions (Logozzo, Lahiri, Fähndrich, Blackshear, PLDI 2014). Regression verification (Godlin & Strichman, DAC 2009) is a parallel formal thread.

**Recent equivalence checking.** ARDiff (Badihi, Akinotcho, Li, Rubin, ESEC/FSE 2020, pp. 13–24) uses iterative abstraction-refinement of common code; per the authors it "outperforms existing method-level equivalence checking techniques by solving 86% of all equivalent and 55% of non-equivalent cases, compared with 47% to 69% for equivalent and 38% to 52% for non-equivalent cases in related work." PASDA (partition-based semantic differencing, 2023) and Datalog-based concurrent-program diffing (2018) are newer variants. Abstract semantic diffing of concurrent programs (Bouajjani, Enea, Lahiri) compares programs by cross-thread interference and data-flow under a parametric abstraction.

### Subtopic 5 — Visualization techniques (the actual rendering of the delta)

This is where the program-analysis and information-visualization communities meet least often, and the biggest opportunity for new tooling.

**Dynamic-graph-visualization foundations.** The authoritative survey is Beck, Burch, Diehl & Weiskopf, "A Taxonomy and Survey of Dynamic Graph Visualization" (*Computer Graphics Forum* 36(1), 2017; earlier EuroVis STAR 2014). It identifies the representation of *time* as the key axis: **animated diagrams** (node-link positions morph across time) vs. **timeline-based** static charts (small multiples, layered/superimposed views). The classic static-graph trade-off (node-link for sparse graphs, adjacency matrix for dense) carries over; hybrid approaches (NodeTrix, MatrixExplorer/MatLink by Henry & Fekete) combine both.

**GraphDiaries (Bach, Pietriga, Fekete, *IEEE TVCG* 20(5):740–754, 2014, DOI 10.1109/TVCG.2013.254)** is the key technique for change perception. It uses **staged animated transitions with change highlighting**: rather than animating everything at once, each transition is decomposed into three sequential stages matching the low-level change types — (1) removal (~300 ms: red halos fade in around departing nodes/edges, which then fade out), (2) layout transformation (~600 ms: surviving nodes slide to new positions with slow-in/slow-out easing; attribute changes also animate here), (3) addition (~300 ms: new elements fade in with blue halos). Removed = red, added = blue, retained = gray. The authors motivate staging directly: "Staged transitions avoid overloading users with too much information. Staging allows them to focus on each type of event in turn." A controlled user study found GraphDiaries "outperforms [existing techniques] in terms of both task time and errors for several of these tasks." This color-and-staging vocabulary maps almost directly onto CFG/PDG delta rendering: added/removed/changed nodes and edges.

**Software-specific evolution visualization.** AniMatrix (VISSOFT 2014) is an interactive matrix-based visualization with staged animation of software-design evolution (added/removed modules and couplings), using exactly the removal→move→modify→addition staging sequence and red/green color coding. Related software-evolution-visualization work includes CVSscan (Voinea, Telea, van Wijk, SoftVis 2005) for code-line evolution, Collberg et al.'s graph-based software-evolution system (SoftVis 2003), "Visualizing project evolution through abstract syntax tree analysis" (Feist, Santos, Watts, Hindle, VISSOFT 2016), and Githru (TVCG 2021) for Git-history analytics. Gleicher's "Considerations for Visualizing Comparison" (*IEEE TVCG* 2018) frames the general design space (juxtaposition/side-by-side, superposition/overlay, explicit-encoding of differences) that any diff visualization must choose among.

**Practical encodings in use today.** Side-by-side (juxtaposed) vs. unified (superposed) diff views; edge/node coloring for added (green/blue), removed (red), changed (yellow/amber); animated morphing between layouts; and matrix representations for dense dependence graphs. Difftastic's side-by-side structural view with aligned changes and move detection is a practical exemplar at the tree level.

### Subtopic 6 — Change impact analysis and program slicing

Program slicing (Weiser, "Program Slicing," ICSE 1981 / *IEEE TSE* 1984) decomposes a program by data- and control-flow so that a slice contains exactly the statements that may affect a chosen slicing criterion. Static slicing over the PDG/SDG (Horwitz–Reps–Binkley) gives conservative over-approximations; dynamic slicing (Korel & Laski, 1988/1990) narrows to a specific execution. Slicing is the mechanism by which an edit's effect is propagated through control and data flow: a forward slice from the changed statements = potentially-affected downstream code.

**Chianti (Ren, Shah, Tip, Ryder, Chesley, OOPSLA 2004, pp. 432–448, DOI 10.1145/1028976.1029012)** is the landmark change-impact tool. It decomposes an edit between two Java versions into a set of interdependent **atomic changes** (added/deleted classes, added/deleted/changed methods, added/deleted fields, and lookup/dynamic-dispatch changes — 16 categories) with prerequisite dependences between them: "an atomic change A1 is dependent on another atomic change A2 if applying A1 to the original version of the program without also applying A2 results in a syntactically invalid program." It builds a **call graph per test** and reports **affected tests** (whose original-version call graph contains a node for a changed/deleted method or an edge matching a lookup change) and, for each, the subset of **affecting changes** responsible. The authors report: "We evaluated Chianti on a year (2002) of CVS data from M. Ernst's Daikon system, and found that, on average, 52% of Daikon's unit tests are affected. Furthermore, each affected unit test, on average, is affected by only 3.95% of the atomic changes."

**Behavioral/dynamic impact.** JDiff feeds coverage/profile estimation and regression selection. Orso, Apiwattanapong & Harrold's "execute-after sequences" (ICSE 2005) is an efficient dynamic impact analysis. UMLDiff (Xing & Stroulia, ASE 2005) does design-level structural change detection to support evolution analysis.

### Subtopic 7 — Notable tools and systems (consolidated)

- **Tree/AST diff:** GumTree (+ gumtree-spoon-ast-diff), ChangeDistiller, Diff/TS, MTDIFF, IJM, srcDiff, Difftastic, Diffsitter, SatDiff, HyperDiff.
- **Source CFG/OO diff:** JDiff (ECFG-based).
- **Dependence/semantic diff:** Semantic Diff (Jackson & Ladd), Horwitz's PRG-based diff, Dex (semantic-graph differencing for C, Raghavan et al.).
- **Behavioral/verification diff:** DSE (Symbolic PathFinder-based), SymDiff (Boogie/Z3), ARDiff, PASDA, Differential Assertion Checking, regression verification.
- **Binary CFG diff (with visualization):** BinDiff (Google/Zynamics), BinHunt, Diaphora, DeepBinDiff, QBinDiff.
- **Change impact:** Chianti, UMLDiff, execute-after sequences.
- **Graph-diff / evolution visualization:** GraphDiaries, AniMatrix, CVSscan, Githru, dynamic-graph visualization toolkits.

## Recommendations

**If your goal is to build a developer-facing CFG/data-flow diff visualizer (e.g., inside an IDE or an LLM-agent code-review system):**

1. **Establish node correspondence first with AST matching.** Use a GumTree-style two-phase matcher (top-down isomorphic anchoring + bottom-up Dice-similarity matching) or ChangeDistiller to get robust, move-aware statement/expression correspondences. This is a solved-enough problem with open-source implementations; do not reinvent it. **Threshold to change approach:** if AST-mapping accuracy on your corpus falls in the known 20–36% error band (per the Fan et al. ICSE 2021 study), invest in language-specific tuning (IJM/srcDiff-style) before building visualization on top.

2. **Lift correspondences to a PDG/SDG and diff at the dependence level.** Project the matched AST nodes onto PDG nodes, then compute added/removed/changed control- and data-dependence edges. This gives you a true control/data-flow delta rather than a syntactic one — the thing Horwitz and Jackson-&-Ladd showed matters. For interprocedural effects, use the SDG with summary edges.

3. **Render with the dynamic-graph-visualization vocabulary.** Adopt GraphDiaries' staged transitions (remove → relayout → add) and the red/green(blue)/gray color code for removed/added/retained nodes and edges. Offer both a **superimposed** (single graph, colored delta) view for small changes and a **juxtaposed side-by-side** view for large ones, following Gleicher's comparison-design guidance. For dense dependence graphs, provide a matrix view (adjacency-matrix diff) as an alternative to node-link.

4. **Add slice-scoped focus.** Let the user pick a changed statement and forward-slice to highlight only the downstream control/data-flow affected by that edit (Weiser/HRB slicing), and backward-slice for root-cause. This is the single most valuable interaction for "how does this edit propagate."

5. **For correctness-critical use, bolt on behavioral diffing selectively.** Where the question is "did behavior actually change," integrate a DSE/SymDiff/ARDiff-style equivalence check on the changed methods (with uninterpreted functions for unchanged callees). Treat it as an expensive, opt-in oracle — these do not scale to whole programs. **Threshold:** only invoke on methods whose PDG delta is non-empty and whose changed statements survive slicing.

**If you only need a fast, practical improvement over line diff today:** adopt Difftastic or GumTree for structural (tree-level) diffs immediately; they are production-ready and language-broad. This gets you move/update awareness without the dependence-graph machinery.

## Caveats
- **Terminology overload.** "Semantic diff" is used for at least three different things: dependence-summary diffing (Jackson & Ladd), PDG-partition classification (Horwitz), and behavioral/equivalence checking (DSE, SymDiff). They differ enormously in cost and guarantees. I have kept them separate above.
- **The core gap is real, not just my framing.** I found abundant literature on tree diffing and on dynamic-graph visualization, but very little that directly visualizes *source-level CFG/PDG deltas* for developers. The best-developed CFG-delta visualizers are binary-analysis tools (BinDiff/BinHunt), whose audience and constraints differ from source editing. Any claim that a polished, general, source-level CFG/data-flow diff visualizer exists as a mainstream product should be treated skeptically.
- **AST-matching accuracy is an unsolved base-layer problem** (20–36% mapping errors reported), which propagates into any dependence-level or visualization layer built on top.
- **Some cited items are venue-ambiguous across sources.** Horwitz's semantic-differences paper is variously cited as PLDI 1990 and *SIGPLAN Notices* 25(6):234–245, 1990 — these are the same work (the PLDI proceedings appeared as that SIGPLAN Notices issue). Diff/TS's exact venue (Hashimoto & Mori 2008) I could not fully pin down from primary sources and report it at lower confidence.
- **Industry claims** (e.g., specific vendor diff features) come partly from vendor documentation and secondary summaries and may reflect marketing; I have flagged BinDiff's closed-source status (free plugin for IDA Pro after Google's 2011 Zynamics acquisition) and noted where descriptions come from tool manuals rather than peer review.
- **Recency:** the newest primary items I verified are the 2024 scalable-GumTree work (Falleri & Martinez, ICSE 2024) and 2023–2024 semantic-diffing papers (PASDA, SatDiff). Developments after mid-2024, including any LLM-based diff-explanation systems, are outside what I could confirm.
